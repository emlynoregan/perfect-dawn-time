Two competing definitions of time.

A mean solar day averages the Sun's progress into uniform 24-hour days. An apparent solar day follows the real Sun. Their difference accumulates and reverses through the seasons. The difference, measured in minutes, is the equation of time.

Two effects, one squiggle.

Orbital eccentricity. Earth moves faster around the Sun near perihelion than aphelion. A sundial therefore gets slightly ahead and behind a uniform clock as the year proceeds.

Axial tilt. The Sun's motion along the ecliptic projects unevenly onto the celestial equator. This produces a stronger, twice-yearly component. Add both and you get the uneven four-lobed curve above.

A USEFUL APPROXIMATION
E ≈ 4°−1 × [y sin(2L) − 2e sin(M) + …]

Here L is the Sun's mean geometric longitude, M its mean anomaly, e Earth's orbital eccentricity, and y = tan²(ε/2), with ε the axial obliquity. The site uses the fuller NOAA approximation, not the truncated expression above. Its E convention is apparent solar time minus mean solar time.

Why PDT needs it.

Longitude tells us what a mean solar clock would say. Latitude and declination tell us how far sunrise is from solar noon. The equation of time then connects that ideal noon to real solar position. Over a year, E contributes a small but visible component to your dawn clock's daily resets — even at the equator.

Of course, an actual PDT clock needs only the sequence of sunrise instants. E is already part of the calculation of those instants; it is not separately added again.
