THE PRINCIPLE / 01

What time is dawn?

Six o'clock, by definition. The maths is simply the machinery needed to make that true.

The coordinate system
φ latitude
λ longitude
δ solar declination

First: what are we anchoring to?

Astronomical sunrise means the instant the Sun's upper edge would first become visible across a level, unobstructed sea-level horizon under standard atmospheric conditions. Its centre is then about 0.8333° below the geometrical horizon. No nearby hills, buildings or unusual weather are included.

THE DEFINING EQUATION
PDT(t, φ, λ) = 06:00 + (t − Sn)

Sn is the most recent calculated sunrise at those coordinates. Take the resulting clock reading modulo 24 hours. At the next sunrise, reset to six again.

Longitude: four minutes per degree.

Earth rotates approximately 360° per mean solar day. East or west changes your solar clock by 4 minutes per degree. This was the whole of my earlier idea, Continuous Local Time: no time zones, just a smooth east–west relationship.

LONGITUDE CORRECTION
L(λ) = 4λ minutes

λ is positive east of Greenwich; west is negative. This changes the clock's offset, but at a stationary point it contributes no changing daily adjustment.

Latitude and the changing seasons.

At the equinox the Sun crosses the equator. At other times its declination δ moves north or south, as far as ±23.44°. The hours from sunrise to solar noon therefore depend on latitude φ and date.

SUNRISE HOUR ANGLE · ANGLES IN DEGREES
H = acos[(sin(−0.8333°) − sinφ sinδ) / (cosφ cosδ)]

H is an angular distance from solar noon to sunrise; each degree corresponds to four minutes of clock time. For an ideal geometric horizon without refraction, this simplifies to H = acos(−tanφ tanδ).

The equation of time.

The Sun is not a uniformly moving clock hand: Earth's orbit is elliptical, and its axis is tilted. The difference between apparent solar time and mean solar time is called E, the equation of time.

APPROXIMATE PDT OFFSET FROM UTC
PDT ≈ UTC + 4λ + 4(H − 90°) + E

All additions are in minutes. This instantaneous approximation is useful for intuition; the implemented clock uses calculated sunrise events, holding the offset fixed until the next dawn rather than continuously updating it. Explore E →

So why does the clock jump?

Let Sn and Sn+1 be consecutive calculated sunrises in UTC. Their separation is not quite 24 hours. The clock ticks with SI seconds between them, then adjusts exactly when the next sunrise occurs.

JUMP AT THE NEXT DAWN
Jn = 86,400 s − (Sn+1 − Sn)

Sunrise earlier than yesterday: jump forwards. Sunrise later: jump backwards. Clock readings in the skipped/repeated interval are discontinuous; UTC is always the unambiguous underlying instant.

What happens near the poles?

Some days have no actual sunrise, and some have no sunset. The site marks these as virtual dawns: when the hour-angle equation has no real solution, it clamps H to 0° in polar night (apparent solar noon) or 180° in polar day (apparent solar midnight). This is an explicit convention, not a claim of an actual sunrise. The real sunrise-to-six promise only applies on days with a sunrise. Approaching those boundaries, daily jumps can become very large.

A note on precision

The demo uses standard NOAA/Meeus-style approximate solar equations and iterates to solve sunrise. Refraction, observer elevation and terrain change real observed sunrise, often by a minute or more. This is a proposed civil-time convention, not a replacement for high-precision ephemerides or UTC synchronization.
